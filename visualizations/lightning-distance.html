How far away was that lightning?

Light reaches you almost instantly; the thunder crawls behind it at about 343 m/s. Count the seconds between the flash and the boom, multiply by the speed of sound, and you've measured the storm's distance without any instrument at all.

Sound travels at roughly 343 m/s in dry air at 20°C — a touch faster in warmer or more humid air, a touch slower in cold or dry air, but 343 m/s is the standard reference figure used here. That's close enough to the folk rule — "5 seconds per mile" (343 m/s × 5 s ≈ 1715 m, against a mile's 1609 m) or "3 seconds per kilometre" (343 m/s × 3 s ≈ 1029 m) — that either is a fine way to estimate a storm's distance while counting on your fingers. Treating the flash as instantaneous isn't a shortcut here, it's honest: light covers a kilometre in about 3.3 microseconds, genuinely negligible next to the seconds thunder takes. The one real approximation is the gauge below — the d = v·Δt formula keeps climbing past what you can actually hear, since atmospheric absorption and upward refraction usually mute thunder beyond roughly 15–20 km, well short of what a 60-second delay would imply. See Thunder and Speed of sound on Wikipedia.

How loud thunder gets isn't a linear relationship with distance. Two separate losses shrink it: geometric spreading, where the sound pressure level falls off with the inverse square of distance — a drop of 6 dB every time the distance doubles, the same rule that governs any point source (Physics LibreTexts) — and atmospheric absorption, a separate loss that's roughly linear in dB per kilometre but strongly frequency-dependent: higher frequencies are absorbed much faster than low ones. That second effect is also why a nearby strike cracks sharply while the same stroke rumbles by the time it travels several kilometres — the crack's higher frequencies get stripped out along the way, leaving mostly the low rumble (Engineering Acoustics: Thunder acoustics). Peak loudness right at the lightning channel is commonly cited around 120–130 dB, among the loudest natural sounds on Earth (Britannica) — and it isn't the same for every strike: a longer or more energetic discharge channel radiates more acoustic energy, so some claps are genuinely louder than others before distance even enters the picture. The estimated loudness figure above uses geometric spreading alone from an assumed 120 dB at 10 m; it leaves out the extra, frequency-dependent loss from atmospheric absorption, so real thunder at longer range usually runs a bit quieter than that number suggests. Put together, the full expression is L(r) = L(r₀) − 20·log₁₀(r/r₀) − α(f)·(r − r₀), where α(f) — the atmospheric absorption coefficient, in dB per unit distance — itself depends on frequency, temperature, humidity and pressure, formalized in the ISO 9613-1 standard as a sum of a classical (viscosity and heat conduction) term plus separate oxygen and nitrogen molecular-relaxation terms; it's this α(f) that decides, at any given distance, how much of each frequency survives — which is why the pitch of thunder itself shifts with distance, not just its loudness (the "pitch" line above reflects that). Measured thunder does have a "standard" pitch: a classic study found a dominant peak around 200 Hz averaged across many recordings (Few, 1967), though a strike's initial crack carries real energy well above that, and by several kilometres out only the lowest rumble, well under 200 Hz, usually survives.
