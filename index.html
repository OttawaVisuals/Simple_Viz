Physics you can see.

Small, honest experiments for concepts that are usually hidden inside numbers. Each page starts with one question, then lets you move the quantities yourself.

Everyday maths

Five pages

Quantities from an ordinary day — a storm, a car, a bicycle, a bookshelf, the view from a beach — that you can check against your own life.

01 / Geometry

Why standing up lets you see farther

Earth curves away beneath you — raise your eye height and watch the visible horizon push out from a few kilometres to thousands.

 02 / Sound

How far away was that lightning?

Time the gap between the flash and the boom, multiply by the speed of sound, and measure a storm without any instrument.

 03 / Motion

Why speed quadruples your stopping distance

Drag the speed slider and watch braking distance grow with the square of speed — and see reaction distance stack on top.

 04 / Gearing

Same legs, different gear

Pick a chainring and a sprocket, hold your cadence steady, and watch one pedal stroke buy either speed or a hill you can actually climb.

 05 / Bending

A shelf that sags

Load the middle of a plank and watch it bow — then turn the same board on its edge and see one cubed term erase almost all of it.

06 / Speed

Earth to the Moon, at real speed

Watch light cross the Earth–Moon gap, and magnify the tiny first part of the same journey for slower travellers.

 07 / Light and distance

You are seeing the past

Choose a planet and compare where its light began with where that planet is now.

 08 / Mass and energy

How much energy is in matter?

Turn an ordinary amount of mass into the energy hidden in E = mc2.

 09 / Gravity

The pull between worlds

Change mass and distance to feel Newton’s inverse-square law take effect.

 10 / Geometry

Two sticks measure a planet

Compare shadow angles at two cities a known distance apart, the way Eratosthenes sized up the Earth around 240 BC.

 11 / Optics

Why the straw looks bent

Bend a ray of light across the boundary into water or glass, then reverse it and watch the same equation cut the light off entirely.

 12 / Propulsion

Why one more passenger costs so much fuel

See why required propellant grows exponentially, not linearly, with the speed change a rocket needs.

 13 / Orbits

Why Mercury's year is 88 days

Slide a planet's distance from the Sun outward and watch its orbital period grow even faster than its distance.
