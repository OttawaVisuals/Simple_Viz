t = d / v

Earth to the Moon, at real speed.

A beam of light crosses the 384,400 km to the Moon in about 1.3 seconds — fast enough to watch, bouncing back and forth below in true, unsped-up time. NASA's Parker Solar Probe, a rifle bullet, a jet airliner, and even sound itself move at their real speed too, just far too slowly to see against that same distance — on this same line they'd barely inch away from Earth, which is why the first 5,000 km is magnified underneath instead.

Distance is the Moon's average distance from Earth; light speed is 299,792 km/s in vacuum, giving t = d/v = 384,400 km ÷ 299,792 km/s = 1.28 s one-way. The lower ruler is not a second trip: it is the first 5,000 km of the route above, magnified about 81× so the slower travellers can be seen moving at all — the accent box above encloses that same 5,000 km on the full-scale line, with the Parker Solar Probe's own faint line drawn right beside light's, the only traveller fast enough to visibly creep even at full scale. Sound and the rest only become visible once magnified, which is exactly why the zoom exists. The speed control multiplies every clock equally; nothing is warped relative to anything else.

Speeds and sources: Parker Solar Probe's 192.2 km/s is its record closest-approach speed near the Sun in December 2024, not one it reaches near Earth — treated here as our fastest machine, hypothetically let loose on this trip (NASA). The rifle bullet's 1 km/s is a typical rifle muzzle velocity, not any one cartridge (Wikipedia). The jet airliner's 0.25 km/s (900 km/h) is a widebody's typical cruising speed (Boeing 747-8). Speed of sound in air, 343 m/s at 20°C (NASA Glenn); in fresh water, 1,481 m/s at 20°C (The Engineering ToolBox) — sound genuinely travels faster through water than a rifle bullet does through air. The “total distance crossed” comparison below uses Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn's average distance from the Sun, not from Earth (232, 795, and 1,511 million km — about 12.9, 44.2, and 84 light-minutes, NASA Goddard): it's a benchmark for the size of the number light has racked up, not a claim about where it's actually headed.
