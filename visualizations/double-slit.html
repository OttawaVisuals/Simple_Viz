What you are seeing

The lines are probability, not little physical tracks. This diagram uses an idealized far-field model so the pattern stays visible while you change it.

What it means — and what it does not

Light is a wave

Young’s original 1801 experiment used light. Two paths combine: in step gives a bright stripe; out of step gives a dark one. Water waves and sound can do this too.

Detections are discrete

With sufficiently weak light, the screen records separate photon-sized events. Over many events, their positions reproduce the same interference pattern.

Measuring the path changes it

Not because a person looks. A device that can record which slit couples to the particle and removes the coherent phase relationship the fringes require.

You can do the wave version at home

You can reproduce the optical interference pattern. A true one-photon-at-a-time version needs specialized low-light detection, so this is an analogue of the original light experiment.

What you need

A low-power laser pointer, a commercial double-slit slide (most reliable), a white wall or paper screen, and a dim room. A diffraction grating is an easier alternative, but makes many slits rather than two.

Try it

  1. Place the slide in the beam.
  2. Put the screen 1–3 m away.
  3. Rotate or move the screen until central and side bright bands are clear.
  4. Measure fringe spacing; it grows with wavelength and screen distance, and shrinks as slits move farther apart.

Safety and limits

Never aim a laser at eyes, reflective surfaces, vehicles, or aircraft. Do not use a high-power laser.

DIY slits made from razor blades or foil are difficult to align and can add unnecessary sharp-object risk. A ready-made slide is safer and more dependable.

Historical note: Thomas Young’s double-slit demonstration (1801) supported the wave theory of light. The particle-by-particle version is a later quantum experiment. Sources: Feynman, Leighton & Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. III, Ch. 1; single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction approximation.
