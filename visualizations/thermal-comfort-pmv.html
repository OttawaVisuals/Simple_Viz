The Fanger PMV/PPD model (ISO 7730, adopted by ASHRAE 55) puts a full body heat balance — metabolic heat in, against respiration, convection, radiation and sweat losses out — onto a −3…+3 comfort scale, then converts that into PPD, the percentage of people predicted to be dissatisfied (PPD = 100 − 95·e−0.03353·PMV4−0.2179·PMV2, never zero — nobody makes everyone happy). A room is compliant when −0.5 ≤ PMV ≤ +0.5 and PPD ≤ 10%, shown as the shaded band on the gauge above. The room is a fixed 10×10 m floor plan with a 2.4 m ceiling — not adjustable, but concrete on purpose, so dragging the person means something (the readout under the gauge states it in metres) and the heat-loss figures below use each wall's real 10×2.4 m face area rather than an arbitrary placeholder. Every exterior surface's temperature is calculated, not set directly. Steady-state conduction gives Tsurface = Tin − (Tin − Tout)·Rsi/Rtotal, where Rtotal adds that surface's own R-value (entered in the imperial units common on Canadian insulation batts, converted internally: 1 = 0.176 m²·K/W) plus a 0.03 m²·K/W exterior air film — the same formula for a wall marked "Exterior" and for a ceiling switched to "Roof", just with that surface's own R-value. Rsi, the interior air-film resistance, depends on which way heat is actually flowing through that surface (ASHRAE Fundamentals Table 1, non-reflective surfaces): walls and windows use 1/8.29 m²·K/W for horizontal flow, and the roof switches between 1/9.26 (indoor warmer than outdoor, heat escaping upward — the usual winter case) and 1/6.13 (outdoor warmer, heat arriving downward, e.g. summer sun on the roof) depending on the sign of Tin−Tout at that moment — buoyancy assists an upward-flowing air film and resists a downward one, which is why the three differ. Windows use their U-value directly, since a U-factor already is the whole-assembly figure: heat flux is U·(Tin−Tout), and the same vertical-surface Rsi fraction of that gives the glass temperature. Window size deliberately does not appear in either formula — a bigger pane isn't colder glass, it's just more of it. What size (and which walls are exterior at all) actually changes: how much of the room's radiant "view" is glazing versus opaque wall versus roof (feeding tr below), and what share of the whole envelope's heat loss the windows carry, both shown in the readout under the gauge. Each window can also be dragged along its own wall — grab it directly in the diagram, or use its wall card's position slider — and position now genuinely matters: standing squarely in front of a window exposes you to (nearly) its full share of that wall's radiant view, while standing off to the side sees progressively more of the opaque wall instead, fading to purely-opaque by about 4 m of lateral offset — another simplified, illustrative falloff rather than a measured solid-angle calculation. Mean radiant temperature (tr) is a weighted average of every wall, window, ceiling and floor temperature, weighted by how much of each you can actually "see" from where you're standing (angle factor) — this page uses a simplified, illustrative angle-factor model based only on distance to each of the four walls, not full solid-angle geometry, but the direction is honest: stand in a corner where two walls are both exterior and both pull tr down at once, which is exactly why corners of old houses feel colder than a single-exterior-wall room even at the same air temperature. The "Show comfort map" toggle above the diagram draws PPD as a topographic map: it re-evaluates the whole PMV/PPD model at ~500 points across the floor, holding walls, air and clothing fixed and varying only position, then traces lines of equal PPD through that grid the same way a contour map traces equal elevation — tightly-packed rings mean comfort changes fast over a short distance (typically near a cold window or corner), and the dashed 10% line specifically marks the ASHRAE 55 compliance boundary. Operative temperature is approximated as the simple average of air and radiant temperature, valid when air speed is low. Clothing (clo) and activity (met) values are standard reference figures from ASHRAE's clothing-insulation tables and metabolic-rate tables, not measurements of any specific outfit or person. The draft, floor-temperature and radiant-asymmetry flags below the gauge are simplified proxies for ASHRAE 55's separate local-discomfort limits (draft rate, floor 19–29°C, radiant asymmetry <5°C for a cool surface) — real assessments measure them directly rather than approximating from the inputs here, and this page can flag more than one wall's asymmetry at once, which a single-number PMV never would.

+− Full methodology, equations and sources

Overview

The page runs ten calculation steps every time a control changes: (1) each exterior wall's opaque surface temperature, (2) each window's surface temperature, (3) the ceiling or roof's surface temperature, (4) mean radiant temperature at the person's position, (5) operative temperature, (6–7) PMV and PPD from Fanger's model, (8) the envelope heat-loss share, (9) three local-discomfort flags, and (10), only when the comfort map is open, PPD re-evaluated on a grid and contoured. All ten are exact re-implementations of published formulas except where explicitly marked "illustrative" below — those are geometric simplifications this page makes so a 2D top-view diagram can respond to position at all, not shortcuts in the thermal physics itself.

Fixed assumptions

Room: 10×10 m floor plan, 2.4 m ceiling height (typical residential), not adjustable — stated explicitly so "5 m from the west wall" means something concrete. Each wall's face area for the heat-loss-share calculation (step 8) is the real 10×2.4 m = 24 m²; the roof/ceiling's face is the full 10×10 m = 100 m² footprint. Person's radiant exposure is evaluated at floor level (z = 0); the model does not vary with standing/seated height. External work done by the body (W in the Fanger equation) is fixed at 0. Relative air speed is floored at 0.001 m/s in the PMV solver to avoid a divide-by-zero at the "0.00 m/s" slider position.

Step 1 — Opaque wall / roof surface temperature

Steady-state 1D conduction, ISO 6946-style: heat flux through the assembly is driven by the total indoor–outdoor temperature difference across the total thermal resistance, and the surface temperature is what's left of that difference after only the interior air film's share is subtracted.

R_total = R_si + R_assembly + R_so q = (T_in − T_out) / R_total T_surface = T_in − q·R_si

R_assembly is the user's R-value slider (entered in imperial ft²·°F·h/Btu, since that's what's printed on Canadian insulation batts) converted with 1 R-value unit = 0.176110 m²·K/W (i.e. RSI = Rimperial / 5.678263, the standard IP→SI factor). R_so = 0.03 m²·K/W is the ASHRAE exterior surface resistance for a 24 km/h (15 mph) winter design wind, from the same table cited below. R_si is direction-dependent — see Step 3.

Step 2 — Window surface temperature

A window's U-value is already the whole-assembly heat transfer coefficient (frame, spacer, gas fill and both surface films combined, per NFRC/ISO 15099 convention), so no separate resistance stack is built for it:

q = U·(T_in − T_out) T_window = T_in − q·R_si,vertical

The same interior film resistance used for vertical walls (Step 3) is applied to back out just the interior glass-surface temperature from the total flux. This is an approximation — a real window's own interior-film fraction of its 1/U depends on its specific frame and edge-of-glass construction, which isn't knowable from a single U-value alone.

Step 3 — Interior air-film coefficients (Rsi)

The interior surface resistance is not one number: air moving by natural convection behaves differently depending on whether it's flowing up, down, or sideways past the surface, so ASHRAE tabulates it by direction. This page selects the entry live, based on the sign of Tin−Tout for the roof (since which way heat is flowing through it changes with the seasons), and uses the fixed vertical value for every wall and window regardless of season.

  • Vertical surfaces (walls, windows), horizontal heat flow: hsi = 8.29 W/(m²·K) → Rsi = 0.1206 m²·K/W
  • Horizontal surface, heat flowing up (roof/ceiling losing heat outward — indoor warmer, the usual winter case): hsi = 9.26 W/(m²·K) → Rsi = 0.1080 m²·K/W
  • Horizontal surface, heat flowing down (roof/ceiling gaining heat inward — outdoor warmer, e.g. a hot sunlit roof in summer): hsi = 6.13 W/(m²·K) → Rsi = 0.1631 m²·K/W

Source: ASHRAE Handbook — Fundamentals, Table 1, "Surface Conductances and Resistances for Air," non-reflective surfaces (ε = 0.90), still air. These figures are unchanged across editions; see the 2001 ASHRAE Fundamentals Handbook, Ch. 25 or the 1997 edition, Ch. 24 (same table, both SI and IP units). The table also lists a sloped-45° case (9.09 up / 7.50 down), not used here since every surface on this page is modeled as either flat-horizontal or vertical.

Step 4 — Mean radiant temperature (tr)

The exact definition of tr is a solid-angle-weighted average of every surface a person can see, using true view/angle factors computed from room geometry. This page uses an illustrative substitute: each of the four walls gets a raw "closeness" weight from the person's fractional distance to it (0 = standing at the wall, 1 = standing at the opposite wall),

raw_wall = 0.06 + 0.16·(1 − distance_to_wall)

ceiling and floor each get a fixed weight of 0.12, and every weight is then divided by the sum of all six so they add to exactly 1. A wall's raw weight is split between its window and its opaque portion by glazing fraction and lateral alignment (Step 5) before that normalization. The weighted fourth-power average (the physically correct way to average radiant temperatures, since radiant exchange itself scales with T4) then gives:

t_r = [ Σ F_i·(T_i + 273)4 ]0.25 − 273

summed over all six weighted surfaces (four walls' opaque+window portions, ceiling/roof, floor). This model captures the right direction of every effect this page demonstrates (closer to a cold surface pulls tr down; two exterior walls compound in a corner) but not true magnitudes — an exact calculation needs each surface's real solid angle from the occupant's actual eye/body position, not just a 1D distance-to-wall proxy.

Step 5 — Window alignment (lateral position along the wall)

A window's share of its wall's raw weight also depends on how directly the person is standing in front of it, not just the room-wide glazing percentage:

alignment = clamp(1 − |along − window_pos| / 0.4, 0, 1) window weight = raw_wall × glazing × alignment

where along is the person's fractional position along that wall's own axis and window_pos is the window's own fractional position (both 0–1, set by dragging the window in the diagram or its wall card's slider). Full exposure directly in front, fading linearly to zero (pure opaque wall) by 0.4 room-widths (4 m in this 10 m room) of lateral offset — a deliberately simple, illustrative falloff, not a measured solid-angle result.

Step 6 — Operative temperature

t_o = (t_a + t_r) / 2

The exact definition weights air and radiant temperature by their respective heat transfer coefficients, t_o = (h_c·t_a + h_r·t_r)/(h_c+h_r); the simple average used here is the standard approximation valid when h_c ≈ h_r, which holds at the low air speeds (≤1 m/s) this page's slider range covers.

Step 7 — PMV (Fanger's model, ISO 7730 reference algorithm)

This is the classic iterative computational form of Fanger's original 1970 heat-balance equation, as codified in ISO 7730 and reproduced in essentially every published PMV implementation (including the CBE Thermal Comfort Tool's reference documentation). Inputs: air temperature ta, mean radiant temperature tr (Step 4), relative air velocity var, relative humidity RH, metabolic rate M (met), clothing insulation Icl (clo), external work W (fixed at 0 here).

p_a = 10·RH·e16.6536 − 4030.183/(t_a+235) (water vapour pressure, Pa) f_cl = 1+1.29·I_cl if I_cl≤0.078 m²K/W, else 1.05+0.645·I_cl (clothing area factor)

Clothing surface temperature tcl and the convective heat-transfer coefficient hc are mutually dependent, so they're solved together by fixed-point iteration (converges in a handful of steps, capped at 150, tolerance 0.00015):

t_cl = 35.7 − 0.028·(M−W) − I_cl·[3.96×10−8·f_cl·((t_cl+273)4−(t_r+273)4) + f_cl·h_c·(t_cl−t_a)] h_c = max(12.1√v_ar , 2.38·|t_cl−t_a|0.25)

Once converged, five heat-loss terms are evaluated (all in W/m²): latent respiration Eres = 0.0000017·M·(5867−pa); dry respiration Cres = 0.0014·M·(34−ta); skin diffusion Ediff = 0.00305·(5733−6.99·(M−W)−pa); regulatory sweating Ersw = 0.42·(M−W−58.15) when M−W>58.15 else 0; radiative loss from clothing Qr = 3.96·fcl·((tcl+273)4−(tr+273)4); convective loss Qc = fcl·hc·(tcl−ta). Their sum is L (shown in the equation box and legend above), and:

PMV = [0.303·e−0.036M + 0.028]·(M − W − L)

Step 8 — PPD

PPD = 100 − 95·e−0.03353·PMV4 − 0.2179·PMV2

A room is treated as ASHRAE 55 compliant when −0.5 ≤ PMV ≤ +0.5 and PPD ≤ 10% (the two conditions are almost, but not exactly, redundant near the boundary).

Step 9 — Envelope heat-loss share

For every exterior wall, Watts through its window and opaque portions are q×area using each surface's own q from Steps 1–2 and the fixed 10×2.4 m wall face (splitting that area by the glazing fraction); if the ceiling is set to Roof, its q×100 m² footprint is added to the opaque total. The reported figure is windows' share of that grand total:

share_window = Σ(q_window·A_window) / [ Σ(q_window·A_window) + Σ(q_opaque·A_opaque) + q_roof·A_roof ] × 100%

Step 10 — Local-discomfort flags

Three simplified proxies for ASHRAE 55's separate local-discomfort limits, evaluated independently of the PMV/PPD headline number:

  • Draft risk: flagged when air speed > 0.15 m/s and air temperature < 22.5°C — ASHRAE 55's own draft-risk threshold for HVAC-driven air movement below that temperature, not a full draft-rate (DR%) calculation.
  • Floor temperature: flagged outside 19–29°C, ASHRAE 55's recommended range for lightly-shod or bare feet.
  • Radiant asymmetry, evaluated per exterior wall: effective asymmetry = (ta − coldest_surface_on_that_wall) × (0.4 + 0.6×proximity), flagged when it exceeds 5°C — ASHRAE 55's limit for a cool surface asymmetry (a warm-ceiling asymmetry limit of 14°C also exists in the standard but isn't checked here, since none of this page's warm-surface scenarios approach it). This proxy can fire for more than one wall at once (see the corner preset), which a single PMV number never surfaces.

Comfort map (PPD contour lines)

With "Show comfort map" enabled, the full PMV/PPD calculation (Steps 4–8) is re-run at every point of a 23×23 grid across the floor, holding walls, ceiling, air and clothing/activity fixed and varying only (x, y). Contour levels are chosen automatically from the grid's actual min/max PPD, similar to how a topographic map's contour interval adapts to the terrain's elevation range: the range is divided into 5 steps, rounded to the nearest "nice" number (1, 2, 5, or 10 × a power of ten), and the ASHRAE 10% line is always added if it falls inside the range. Lines are traced through the grid with the standard marching squares algorithm (linear interpolation along each grid cell's edges, one of the two ambiguous "saddle" cases resolved with a fixed diagonal convention). If PPD varies by less than 0.05 percentage points across the whole floor, no contours are drawn and the page states the range directly instead — a flat field is itself the finding, not a rendering failure.

Reference tables (clothing & activity)

Clothing insulation (Icl, clo): T-shirt & shorts 0.3 · Light summer wear 0.5 · Shirt & trousers 0.7 · Business suit 1.0 · Suit + sweater 1.3 · Heavy winter clothing 1.7. Standard reference figures from ASHRAE's clothing-insulation tables, not a measurement of any specific garment.

Metabolic rate (M, met; 1 met = 58.15 W/m²): Reclining 0.8 · Seated, relaxed 1.0 · Sedentary office work 1.1 · Standing, light work 1.4 · Walking ~1.1 m/s 2.0. From ASHRAE's metabolic-rate tables.
