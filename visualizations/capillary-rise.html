Two questions, two ideal equations

How high can it rise?

h∞ = 2γ cos θ⁄ρgr

At equilibrium, upward capillary pressure balances the column’s weight. Halve the tube radius and the final height doubles.

Assumes a clean, uniform, vertical circular tube; constant surface tension; and a stable contact angle.

How fast does it rise?

dh⁄dt = r²⁄8μh · (2γ cos θ⁄r − ρgh)

This Lucas–Washburn form balances capillary pull against viscous resistance and gravity. The rise is fast first, then slows as both height and flow resistance grow.

Ignores the very first inertial moment. μ is dynamic viscosity; a more viscous liquid rises more slowly.

Test capillarity at home

The most reliable home setup is a narrow gap between two clean pieces of glass. It behaves much more like the ideal model than a paper towel does.

Make a controlled gap

Use two microscope slides or small, uncoated flat glass pieces. Put equal thin tape spacers along both sides, clamp the stack with binder clips, and stand the bottom edge in coloured water.

Handle glass carefully; do not use cracked glass or exposed sharp edges.

Measure a real height

  1. Place a ruler behind the glass.
  2. Wait until the rise is nearly steady.
  3. Measure the height above the reservoir.
  4. Repeat with a thinner or thicker spacer.

For parallel plates, use the gap b instead of tube radius: h ≈ 2γ cosθ/(ρgb).

Compare liquids honestly

Try coloured water, dilute dish-soap water, and vegetable oil. Record both the final height and time to reach it. Oil and soap mixtures vary by product and may wet glass differently, so treat the model as a comparison, not a precise forecast.

Paper towel or coffee filters are a useful extension for speed: fit x² = Ct from your own first run instead of pretending their tangled pores are one tube.

Values shown are illustrative room-temperature estimates: water γ=72.8 mN/m, μ=1.00 mPa·s; dish-soap mixture γ≈28 mN/m, μ≈1.2 mPa·s; vegetable oil γ≈32 mN/m, μ≈60 mPa·s. Contact angle and viscosity depend on the exact liquid and glass cleanliness. Jurin’s law and the gravity-corrected Lucas–Washburn model are idealizations, stated here so measurements can test their limits.
