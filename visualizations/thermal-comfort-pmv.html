The Fanger PMV/PPD model (ISO 7730, adopted by ASHRAE 55) puts a full body heat balance — metabolic heat in, against respiration, convection, radiation and sweat losses out — onto a −3…+3 comfort scale, then converts that into PPD, the percentage of people predicted to be dissatisfied (PPD = 100 − 95·e−0.03353·PMV4−0.2179·PMV2, never zero — nobody makes everyone happy). A room is compliant when −0.5 ≤ PMV ≤ +0.5 and PPD ≤ 10%, shown as the shaded band on the gauge above. The wall and window surface temperatures are calculated, not set directly. Steady-state conduction gives Tsurface = Tin − (Tin − Tout)·Rsi/Rtotal, where Rsi = 0.12 m²·K/W is the standard interior air-film resistance and Rtotal adds the wall's own R-value (converted from the imperial units on the slider) plus a 0.03 m²·K/W exterior air film. Windows are simpler, since a U-value already is the whole-assembly figure: heat flux is just U·(Tin−Tout), and the same Rsi fraction of that gives the interior glass temperature. Window size deliberately does not appear in either formula — a bigger pane isn't colder glass, it's just more of it. What size actually changes: how much of the room's radiant "view" is glazing versus opaque wall (feeding tr below), and what share of the wall assembly's total heat loss the window carries, both shown in the readout under the gauge. Mean radiant temperature (tr) is the piece that makes the room-box worth dragging a person around in: it's a weighted average of the ceiling, floor, window, exterior-wall and interior-wall temperatures, weighted by how much of each one you can actually "see" from where you're sitting (angle factor). This page uses a simplified, illustrative angle-factor model — a real one needs the exact geometry and distance to every surface — but the direction is honest: standing closer to a cold window increases its weight in tr and pulls your comfort down, even though the thermostat (air temperature) hasn't moved at all. Operative temperature is approximated as the simple average of air and radiant temperature, valid when air speed is low. Clothing (clo) and activity (met) values are standard reference figures from ASHRAE's clothing-insulation tables and metabolic-rate tables, not measurements of any specific outfit or person. The draft, floor-temperature and radiant-asymmetry flags below the gauge are simplified proxies for ASHRAE 55's separate local-discomfort limits (draft rate, floor 19–29°C, radiant asymmetry <5°C for a cool surface) — real assessments measure them directly rather than approximating from the inputs here. R-values are entered in the imperial units (ft²·°F·h/Btu) common on Canadian insulation batts and converted internally (1 = 0.176 m²·K/W).
