Escape velocity strictly describes an unpowered object — it's only ever a meaningful checkpoint once thrust stops. That's exactly how it's used here: the verdict compares speed to vesc only from engine cutoff onward, when the rocket is coasting like anything thrown. During the burn itself it isn't racing that number at all; thrust is fighting gravity directly (a = F/m − GM/r², with g weakening as altitude increases and m shrinking as fuel drains) — a rocket first needs thrust greater than its own weight just to leave the ground (F > m₀GM/r²), which the ion-drive preset fails outright: real ion engines produce thrust measured in millinewtons, nowhere near enough to lift anything off a planet, however efficient their exhaust velocity. This page also treats the whole rocket as one stage — the same simplification the rocket equation page uses — so even the Saturn V default (a real rocket that did reach the Moon) falls back well short of escaping here: its actual first stage only ever had to hand off to two more stages above it, each shedding dead weight before igniting. That's the real "tyranny of the rocket equation" at work, not a bug in this model. Once the engine does cut off it's a coast: specific energy ε = ½v² − GM/r is conserved, ε ≥ 0 means escape, and ε < 0 gives an exact apex altitude (rapex = −GM/ε) where it falls back. The energy-budget bar shows this as one number: how much of the planet's potential-energy well (GM/r₀, the same quantity under the root in vesc) has been paid off by height climbed plus speed gained. The coast phase's on-screen climb rate is stylized to fit one frame — real time-to-apex or time-to-escape isn't shown — but every number and the KE/PE split stay exact throughout. Ignores atmospheric drag, staging, and any sideways/orbital motion: a real launch goes mostly sideways early on, this page only ever goes straight up. Exhaust velocities (solid 2,600 m/s, kerosene/LOX 3,000 m/s, hydrogen/LOX 4,400 m/s, ion 30,000 m/s) match the rocket equation page for consistency; rocket mass/thrust figures for the presets are sourced to each vehicle's public specifications, rounded. A real check against this page's own claims: Artemis II's SLS core stage cuts off around 161 km altitude at about 7.6 km/s — nowhere near Earth's 11.19 km/s escape velocity, and it isn't meant to be; that speed only needs to reach a parking-orbit-class trajectory. The actual push toward the Moon comes later, in a separate trans-lunar injection burn by the upper stage, which (using Artemis I's published figures for the same mission architecture) raised Orion from about 7.8 km/s to about 10.1 km/s — still under Earth's escape velocity. Reaching the Moon doesn't require literally escaping Earth's gravity in the two-body sense this page models: it requires a highly elliptical trajectory timed to meet the Moon, a lower-energy, three-body problem this single-body vesc check doesn't capture — which is exactly why Artemis's real trajectory (horizontal insertion, a coast, a second burn) isn't offered as a preset here.
