Hagen–Poiseuille

Why the straw gives up.

Push a fluid through a tube and it fights back with viscous drag. Here's the equation behind that — and why one setup below famously failed on live TV.

The Hagen–Poiseuille equation shown above describes slow, steady (laminar) flow through a round tube, where η is viscosity, L is length, r is inner radius (half the diameter set above), and Q is a fixed, representative flow rate. It's exact for milkshake and honey across this page's whole slider range — both stay laminar (Reynolds number well under 2,300) even at the narrowest, longest setting. Water can leave that regime: at the narrow end of the diameter slider its Reynolds number passes 4,000, so the pressure shown there blends in a turbulent-flow correction (Darcy–Weisbach with an empirical friction factor) rather than extrapolating the laminar formula past where it's valid — the result box flags it ("turbulent flow, Re≈…") whenever this is happening. Air is left on the plain laminar formula here regardless: at the pressures needed to push it through something straw-narrow, air's density changing along the tube (compressibility) is a bigger source of error than turbulence alone, and fixing that needs a different equation entirely, not just a friction-factor correction. The scale's limits are rough estimates of what a person can sustain by mouth, not hard numbers, drawn from clinical measurements of maximal mouth pressure (the same PImax/PEmax test used to check respiratory-muscle strength, not a drinking-straw study specifically). Sucking and blowing are not the same limit: healthy adults typically manage on the order of 10 kPa sucking (PImax) but close to double that blowing, around 20 kPa (PEmax), since the muscles used to exhale are stronger than the ones used to inhale — which is why air's limit is set higher than water's, milkshake's, or honey's above. The gauge below uses a log scale (each major tick is 1,000× the last), since achievable pressures span nine orders of magnitude from a light sip to a seized industrial line. This models horizontal flow only — a straw held upright also fights gravity, and no amount of suction can lift water more than about 10 m, since that alone needs a full atmosphere of pressure difference. It's also the reason a long drinking straw is so much harder to use than a garden hose of similar length: on Taskmaster, comedian Tim Vine taped a chain of drinking straws together and couldn't blow a candle out through them, while a garden hose of the same length worked fine — try both presets above. Real chained drinking straws also kink at their joints, choking flow by a mechanism this equation doesn't capture, likely a bigger factor in Tim Vine's attempt than viscosity alone.
