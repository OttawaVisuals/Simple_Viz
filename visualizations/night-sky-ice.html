Radiative sky cooling

How a desert night can make ice

The air can stay above freezing while a shallow pool loses infrared heat through a clear, dry sky. Ancient Persian ice makers used the cold sky, shade walls and long winter nights to freeze water without a machine.

This is an intentionally bounded radiation-first model, not a reconstruction of one exact historical night. It follows a 100 mm water layer for 11 hours, starting at the air temperature, with a sky view factor of 0.7 and a fixed sheltered convection coefficient of 4 W/m²K. Clear-sky emissivity uses Brutsaert’s 1975 relation, εsky = 1.24(ea/Ta)1/7 with vapour pressure in hPa; cloud cover is a simple interpolation toward an opaque sky. The simulation includes water’s heat capacity and the 333.7 kJ/kg latent heat of fusion, but omits evaporation and ground conduction so the radiative mechanism stays visible. Those omissions pull in opposite directions: a detailed UCL yakhchāl model found sky radiation was the dominant cooling term, evaporation added substantial cooling, and warmer ground added heat. Its historical and simulated system was Iranian/Persian, not Egyptian. “Deep space” is useful shorthand, but the water actually exchanges radiation with the atmosphere and the infrared window through it; the displayed effective sky temperature packages that downwelling radiation into one understandable number.
