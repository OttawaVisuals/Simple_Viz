What is a vacuum?

A vacuum is a region with fewer gas particles and therefore less pressure. It does not pull: surrounding higher-pressure air does the pushing — enough to raise water about 10.3 m (34 ft) at sea level.

A “perfect” vacuum is a limit

In theory it contains no particles at all: 0 Pa absolute pressure. Real vacuums contain a few particles, and the best laboratory vacuums are extraordinarily sparse rather than literally empty. Space is also not perfectly empty.

Why Earth’s air does not drift away

Gravity continuously pulls atmospheric molecules toward Earth. Fast molecules can escape, especially light gases high up, but most nitrogen and oxygen molecules travel far below Earth’s 11.2 km/s escape speed. The atmosphere thins gradually; it has no sharp edge against space.

Method: force is pressure difference × area. The liquid height uses ΔP = ρgh for fresh water at 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s²; it ignores boiling, pipe losses, and surface effects. Read more: vacuum and Earth’s atmosphere.
