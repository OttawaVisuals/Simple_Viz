Mean vs. median

The average lies, a little.

This room is illustrative, not a real survey — twenty-five net-worth values were chosen to already skew right (a common, real shape for wealth and income data), then a twenty-sixth person's net worth is set by the slider above. The mean is the plain arithmetic average, Σx⁄n; the median is the middle value once everyone is sorted (the average of the two middle values here, since twenty-six is even), calculated with the same linear-interpolation method most statistics software uses for percentiles (numpy's default, Excel's PERCENTILE.INC). Because the median only asks "who's in the middle position," moving the wealthiest neighbor's net worth up or down leaves it almost untouched — the mean, which has to average in that value directly, moves with it. Real household wealth is far more concentrated than this toy room: median and mean U.S. net worth routinely differ by a factor of five or more in the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, for exactly this reason — a relatively small number of very wealthy households pull the average far above where most people actually sit. See also: wealth inequality. The horizontal axis uses a log scale (each labelled step is 100× the last) because the room's net worth spans up to eight orders of magnitude once a billionaire moves in — a linear axis would crush all twenty-five ordinary neighbors into a single point at the left edge.
